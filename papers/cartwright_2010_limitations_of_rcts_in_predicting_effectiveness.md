# Cartwright & Munro (2010), "The limitations of randomized controlled trials in predicting effectiveness"

**Links:** https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2753.2010.01382.x · accepted version: LSE Research Online (summary only)

Journal of Evaluation in Clinical Practice 16(2):260–266 (LSE accepted version). Munro is a child-protection and social-work scholar.

- **Claim:** RCTs are good at showing that an intervention worked *in the trial population*, but alone they are a weak basis for predicting effectiveness elsewhere.
- **Causal principles are local:** they hold for populations with a particular causal structure. An RCT tells you the average effect *in that structure*.
- **Capacities and support factors:** an intervention has a *capacity* to contribute to an outcome, but whether it does depends on other factors being present and on the absence of interferers.
- **Use in practice:** combine RCT evidence with knowledge of the mechanisms and of the target context. Look for evidence that the same capacities are at work and that the support factors are present.
- **Example domain:** social work and child protection, where context varies enormously.
- **Message to evidence-based practice:** don't throw out RCTs, but stop treating "what works" lists as transferable recipes.

**Teaching hooks**
- Pair with Wohlin's evidence profile: even "strong evidence" about X there is only circumstantial evidence about X here.
- Bridge to Tue S5a (claim–evidence): a *generalisation* claim needs its own evidence.
