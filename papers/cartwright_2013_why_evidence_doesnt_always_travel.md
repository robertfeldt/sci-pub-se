# Nancy Cartwright (2013), "Knowing what we are talking about: why evidence doesn't always travel"

**Links:** https://doi.org/10.1332/174426413X662581 (summary only; PDF not redistributable)

Evidence & Policy 9(1):97–112.

- **Question:** when is a well-established study result ("it produced the outcome *there*") evidence that the policy will produce it *here*?
- **Answer:** two further facts must be supported by evidence:
  - (a) the policy can play the **same causal role widely**, widely enough to cover both there and here;
  - (b) a **complete set of support factors** needed for it to operate is present for at least some individuals here.
- **"There" and "here" must be firmly distinguished.** Rigour of the study (internal validity) says nothing by itself about travel.
- **Warns against vague "similarity"** claims ("the populations are similar"). Similar in *what*? Similar in the causally relevant respects, which you need to know.
- **Implication for evidence hierarchies:** they rank how well a study establishes the result *there*. They don't rank relevance for *here*. You need different, often non-RCT, evidence (mechanisms, local knowledge, theory) for (a) and (b).

**Teaching hooks**
- The cleanest short statement of Cartwright's argument. Use its two conditions as a slide.
- Critique of hierarchies and pyramids (the Kitchenham ladder, GRADE starting from "RCT = high") from a philosophy-of-science angle.
- Good discussion prompt: "What evidence would convince you that a result from Google transfers to a 10-person Norwegian consultancy?"
