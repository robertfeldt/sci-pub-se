# Applying GRADE-CERQual to qualitative evidence synthesis findings, paper 2: how to make an overall CERQual assessment of confidence and create a Summary of Qualitative Findings table

Lewin S, Bohren M, Rashidian A, et al. (14 authors). *Implementation Science* 2018;13(Suppl 1):10. https://doi.org/10.1186/s13012-017-0689-2 (open access; PMC: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5791047/)

## TLDR
- **Purpose:** the practical "end point" of CERQual: going from review findings to an overall confidence rating per finding, presented in an Evidence Profile and a Summary of Qualitative Findings (SoQF) table. Placed before the component papers so readers see how the component judgments are used.
- **Step 1: write a summary of each review finding.** Make it explicit (spell out hypothesised connections rather than implying them); include context such as countries only if it is part of the finding; write for the end users. Balance splitting into many narrow findings vs a few broad ones that hide variation. Worked examples show how to improve vague, overly contextual or implicit summaries.
- **Step 2: decide which findings to assess.** Ideally all of them, and do it while developing the findings, since it's not always predictable which will matter for a decision. Justify any exclusions. Also decide the level of interpretation (descriptive findings vs broader theory); experience with interpretive findings is still limited.
- **Step 3: assess each finding on each component** using four standard levels of concern: no or very minor, minor, moderate, serious. Describe the concerns explicitly.
  - **No numerical scoring:** it gives a false sense of precision; these are judgments made transparent.
  - **Start high, rate down:** every finding starts at *high confidence* (a reasonable representation unless something weakens that) and is rated down for concerns. Typically at least one level per component with serious concerns; several minor or moderate concerns may together justify one level.
  - **Equal weights:** components are currently weighted equally, for lack of evidence otherwise.
  - **Avoid double downgrading:** e.g. thin data can affect both coherence and adequacy; count it once.
  - **Mixed concerns across studies:** pick the worst, the best, or a "middle ground", and say which.
  - **Iterative:** assessing coherence may lead to splitting or rewording a finding.
- **Who assesses:** ideally the review team on its own findings, through discussion among at least two members. Variation between assessors is expected; the structure and transparent reporting should make it more reliable than intuitive judgment. Applying CERQual to someone else's synthesis is possible but hard and time-consuming.
- **Three layers of presentation:** (1) full findings in the results section; (2) the *Evidence Profile*: summary, per-component judgments with explanations (standard phrases provided), overall assessment, its explanation, contributing studies and their contexts; (3) the *SoQF table*: summary, overall confidence, explanation, contributing studies. The SoQF table is the entry point for decision makers but cannot stand alone.
- **Include all findings, including very low confidence ones**, ordered by theme, setting, priority or confidence level. Low adequacy means *too little data*, not that the phenomenon is rare. If no data were found for a pre-specified phenomenon, report that.
- **Worked examples:** mistreatment of women during childbirth ("use of force": 15 studies, 10 countries → high confidence; "physical restraint": 2 studies with thin data → very low) and lay health worker programmes (findings rated moderate or low with reasons).
- **Implications:** moderate, low or very low confidence signals where more or better primary research is needed. SoQF tables can feed Evidence-to-Decision frameworks alongside GRADE evidence on benefits, harms and resource use.
