# Mary Shaw (2003), "Writing Good Software Engineering Research Papers" (minitutorial)

**Links:** https://doi.org/10.1109/ICSE.2003.1201262 · author copy: https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~Compose/shaw-icse03.pdf

*ICSE 2003*. Based on analysing the abstracts of all ~300 ICSE 2002 submissions and observing the PC discussions.

- **Three questions every paper must answer:**
  1. *What, precisely, was your contribution?* (Which question did you answer, why should the reader care?)
  2. *What is your new result?* (What new knowledge, how is it different from and better than prior work?)
  3. *Why should the reader believe your result?* (Which standard applies, what concrete evidence shows the result satisfies the claim?)
- **SE has no shared research paradigm** like physics experiments or double-blind drug trials, so authors must make their paradigm explicit.
- **Three-dimensional space** (Tables 1, 3, 5):
  - **Question types:** method or means of development; method for analysis/evaluation; design/evaluation of a particular instance; generalisation or characterisation; feasibility/exploration.
  - **Result types:** procedure or technique (must be operational, "not advice"); qualitative/descriptive model; empirical model; analytic model; tool or notation; specific solution/prototype; report.
  - **Validation types:** analysis; evaluation; experience (used by someone other than the author); example (toy vs real); persuasion; blatant assertion.
- **ICSE 2002 numbers:** 298–300 submissions, 43 accepted (14%).
  - *Questions:* "method for development" was most common (48% of submissions, 13% acceptance); "method for analysis" was accepted at a higher rate (20%).
  - *Validation:* **28% of abstracts gave no indication of validation** (7% acceptance); **persuasion: 0 of 25 accepted** ("persuasion was not persuasive"); analysis (23%), experience (24%) and examples (20%) did best.
- **What PCs look for:** evidence that the idea helps *someone else*; validation related to the claim (claim performance → validate performance); honest comparison with prior art; following the rules of the analysis technique used. A "controlled experiment" needs more than data collection, and a "case study" more than anecdotes.
- **Combining question, result and validation:** not every combination works (don't validate a formal model by field study). Choosing validation is the hard part. Don't blindly copy last year's paradigm for a different problem.
- **Abstract template:** 2–3 sentences on the state of the art and the problem → 1–2 on the contribution → 1–2 on the specific result and main idea → 1 on how it's demonstrated or defended.
- **Teaching hooks:** the three questions double as a clinic checklist; "persuasion 0%" makes a memorable slide; the validation types complement Wohlin's evidence profile (paper level vs piece-of-evidence level). It's old (2003): ask what has changed (empirical standards, artifacts, LLM evaluations).
