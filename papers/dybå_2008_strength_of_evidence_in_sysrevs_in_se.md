# Strength of Evidence in Systematic Reviews in Software Engineering

Dybå T, Dingsøyr T. *Proc. 2nd ACM-IEEE Int. Symposium on Empirical Software Engineering and Measurement (ESEM 2008)*, pp. 178–187. https://doi.org/10.1145/1414004.1414034

## TLDR
- **Core question:** systematic reviews are only as good as the evidence they rest on, and SE primary studies are often of poor quality. So how much confidence can users place in a review's conclusions? The paper surveys systems from other fields (mainly medicine) and illustrates them with the authors' own systematic review of agile software development (33 primary studies).
- **Three things a rigorous SE review needs:** (1) explicit quality assessment of each primary study, (2) a grading of the strength of the *total body of evidence*, and (3) explicit discussion of the review's own limitations.
- **Integrative vs interpretive synthesis:** integrative reviews (e.g. meta-analysis) pool well-specified data; interpretive reviews (e.g. meta-ethnography) build higher-order concepts from qualitative studies. Since much SE research is qualitative and observational, the authors expect many SE reviews to be interpretive.
- **Assessing primary studies:**
  - Quality = how well design, conduct and analysis prevent bias (internal validity); external validity depends on the review's purpose.
  - Key bias-related features from medicine: randomisation, blinding, loss to follow-up. Blinding is generally impossible in human-intensive SE tasks, but partial blinding (allocation, marking, data analysis) is possible. Five bias types: selection, performance, attrition, detection, reporting.
  - Risk: with short conference papers you often assess the *quality of reporting* rather than the quality of the research.
- **Their 11-criterion quality checklist** (inspired by CASP and Kitchenham et al.), covering reporting, rigour, credibility and relevance, scored yes/no. In the agile review, only 8 of 33 studies had an appropriate recruitment strategy, 10 had a comparison group, and just 1 considered the researcher–participant relationship (researcher bias).
- **Replications by others matter:** of 15 differentiated replications of SE experiments, 7 of 8 by the original authors confirmed the original results, versus only 1 of 7 by other researchers.
- **Evidence hierarchies** (SRs and randomised experiments on top, expert opinion at the bottom) are criticised because randomised experiments are often infeasible and observational studies can sometimes give better evidence.
- **GRADE (from the GRADE Working Group, medicine) is proposed for SE:** quality of a body of evidence = confidence that an estimate of effect is correct; four levels (high / moderate / low / very low); randomised trials start high, observational studies low; rate down for study limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecise/sparse data, reporting bias; rate up for strong association, dose-response, or when all plausible confounders would reduce the effect. Expert opinion is *not* evidence but an interpretation of evidence.
- **SE-specific readings of GRADE factors:** e.g. *indirectness* when studies use students instead of experienced professionals, small artificial tasks instead of large industrial systems, or defect counts as surrogates for quality; *underestimation of effect* when only novices got the treatment but still did better.
- **First application of GRADE in SE (the agile review):** mostly observational studies → start low; serious quality limitations, inconsistent results and major uncertainty about directness (mostly XP, often students or novices, few direct comparisons) → overall strength of evidence **very low**. Any estimate of agile benefits from the research at the time was very uncertain.
- **Assessing reviews themselves:** reporting standards such as QUOROM (meta-analyses of randomised trials) and MOOSE (reviews of observational studies); MOOSE seen as especially relevant for SE, plus a flow diagram of study selection. The agile review's own limitations: selection bias from non-standardised SE keywords, and data-extraction inaccuracy due to poor reporting in primary studies.
