# Ten simple rules for structuring papers

Mensh B, Kording K. *PLOS Computational Biology* 13(9):e1005619, 2017. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005619 (open access, CC BY 4.0).

## TLDR
- **Premise:** writing is communication, so the reader's experience comes first. Structure the paper at every scale (sentence, paragraph, section, whole paper) around how readers actually consume it. Editors want significance, reviewers want justified conclusions, and readers want the conceptual message fast.
- **Principles (Rules 1–4):**
  1. **Focus on one central contribution, and say it in the title.** Success means readers can still describe your main contribution a year later. Papers with several contributions are less convincing about each one. This "Rule of One" is the hardest to follow.
  2. **Write for flesh-and-blood humans who don't know your work.** You are the world's least qualified judge of how your text reads to a naive reader. Define terms, avoid abbreviations, and respect working memory.
  3. **Use context–content–conclusion (C-C-C)** at every scale. Whole paper: introduction = context, results = content, discussion = conclusion. Paragraph: the first sentence sets the topic, the last one gives the conclusion. A missing context makes readers ask "why was I told that?"; a missing conclusion makes them ask "so what?". Don't structure the paper by the chronology of your work.
  4. **Optimise logical flow:** avoid zig-zag (treat each subject in one place; only the central idea recurs) and use parallelism. Reuse the same word for the same concept, because synonyms make readers wonder whether you mean something different.
- **The parts of a paper (Rules 5–8):**
  5. **Abstract: tell a complete story.** Context narrows to the gap; "Here we" gives approach and results; the conclusion answers the gap and states its broader significance. The commonest mistake is presenting results before the reader can understand them.
  6. **Introduction: why the paper matters.** Progressively narrower paragraphs (field gap → subfield gap → the gap you fill), then a final paragraph summarising what the paper does. No broad literature review beyond the motivation.
  7. **Results: a sequence of declarative statements, backed by figures,** that logically build up the central claim. Use them as subsection headers and figure titles. Each paragraph poses a question and ends by answering it, which makes the paper easy to fact-check.
  8. **Discussion:** how the gap was filled, the limitations of the interpretation, and the relevance to the field. Pre-empt criticism.
- **Process (Rules 9–10):**
  9. **Spend time where it matters:** title, abstract, figures and outline are read far more than the methods. Outline with one informal sentence per planned paragraph before wordsmithing.
  10. **Get feedback to reduce, reuse and recycle the story.** Don't get attached to text; rewriting a paragraph is often faster than editing it. Vague, unenthusiastic reviews usually mean the reviewers missed the big picture; specific feedback points to a gap in a paragraph's logic. Repay colleagues by reading their drafts.
- **Table 1** pairs each rule with a sign that it is being violated, e.g. "readers cannot give a 1-sentence summary" (Rule 1) or "readers do not agree with your conclusion" (Rule 7).

## Teaching hooks
- Rule 7 is the writing side of the course's claim–evidence analysis: if every results paragraph ends in a claim, those claims can be checked against the evidence.
- Use Table 1 as a self-review checklist for your own paper, and as a lens when reviewing: which rule does the paper break?
- Pairs with Shaw 2003: Shaw says *what* an SE paper must establish; Mensh & Kording say how to structure it so readers get it.
