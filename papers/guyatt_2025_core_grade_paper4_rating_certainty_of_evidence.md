# Core GRADE 4: rating certainty of evidence: risk of bias, publication bias, and reasons for rating up certainty

Guyatt G, Wang Y, Eachempati P, et al. (14 authors). *BMJ* 2025;389:e083864. https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-083864

## TLDR
- **Bias** = systematic deviation from the true effect of an intervention on an outcome in a population. Randomised trials start at high certainty; non-randomised studies of interventions start at low because of residual confounding.
- **Step 1: classify each study as overall low or high risk of bias** (a simplification of a continuum, with close calls acknowledged). There is no definitive threshold for how many high-risk items make a study high risk; one or two items is reasonable, more is questionable. State the threshold and rationale explicitly.
- **Instruments for randomised trials:** many exist; some mix in items GRADE treats as indirectness or imprecision (risk of double counting). Cochrane's RoB 2 is rigorous but complex, with low inter-rater reliability; the new ROBUST-RCT (6 core + 8 optional items, two-step "safeguard present?" then "did its absence cause bias?") best fits Core GRADE's simplicity.
- **Instruments for non-randomised studies:** the simpler Newcastle-Ottawa scale or CLARITY's modifications, or ROBINS-I (v1/v2). ROBINS-I builds residual confounding into the risk-of-bias assessment instead of the low starting point; it is often misapplied, slow and hard to use, so it is less aligned with Core GRADE.
- **Case series / single-arm trials** lack a concurrent comparison, so they almost always end as very low certainty. Exception: harms that can only occur in treated people (e.g. colonoscopy complications), where a large single-arm study can give high-certainty harm estimates.
- **Risk of bias can differ by outcome** within one study (e.g. blinding matters little for all-cause mortality but much for subjective outcomes); reviews should state that they considered this.
- **Step 2: rate across the body of evidence (flow chart):**
  - High-risk studies *dominate* (e.g. >65% or ≥55% of meta-analysis weight) → pool all and rate down, unless the direction of bias means it cannot explain the result.
  - *Appreciable* low-risk evidence (≥35–45% of weight) with *different* results from high-risk studies → use only the low-risk studies, don't rate down.
  - Low- and high-risk studies give *similar* results → pool all, don't rate down: risk of bias is only a *risk*, not proof that bias occurred.
- **Consider the direction of bias:** if bias would have made an observed effect smaller, or an observed null effect larger, it strengthens rather than weakens the inference, so there is no reason to rate down.
- **Publication bias:** caused by authors not submitting, editors and reviewers rejecting negative results, and commercial funders suppressing them (e.g. reboxetine: published data overestimated benefit by up to 115%; FDA antidepressant trials). Rate down when unpublished studies are known to exist with sizeable samples, or when the evidence consists of small, mostly industry-sponsored studies.
- **Detecting publication bias is guesswork:** comprehensive searches (registries, regulatory and non-English databases) help but rarely change much; funnel-plot asymmetry has alternative explanations ("small-study effects"); statistical tests (Egger, Begg) have false-positive and false-negative problems and need ≥10 studies. Report the domain as *undetected* or *strongly suspected*.
- **Selective outcome reporting:** unreported outcomes are treated as publication bias; cherry-picking among time points, measures or analyses is treated as risk of bias in the individual study (particularly a concern for non-randomised studies without pre-registered protocols).
- **Rating up (non-randomised studies only, and only if not already rated down):**
  - *Large effect:* relative risk >2 or <0.5 → consider +1; >5 or <0.2 → consider +2, because confounding is unlikely to explain effects that large. Rapid onset or a relentless downhill course without treatment strengthen the case.
  - *Dose-response gradient:* increases confidence in causality, but beware confounded gradients (coffee vs pancreatic cancer turned out to be smoking).
